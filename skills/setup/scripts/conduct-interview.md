# Work History Interview

The goal is to end up with far more material than the CV contains, so that every future application can *select* from real evidence instead of rephrasing the same summary. A CV is what the candidate already decided to show; this interview is everything they could have shown.

Read the CV first. Note what each role claims, what carries a number, and what is vague — "improved conversion", "large team", "drove growth" are all placeholders for something specific.

## Order and depth

Most recent role first. Depth should follow relevance, not chronology: a lot on the last two roles, less as you go back, almost nothing on anything over ten years old or clearly irrelevant to where they are heading. Ask before skipping — sometimes the oldest role is the one that matters.

## Per role

**The company.** What did it actually do? What stage and size when they joined? Who were the competitors? An application cannot describe scope credibly without this.

**The mandate.** What were they hired to fix or build? What state were things in on day one? How did the role change over their tenure? The gap between "what I was hired for" and "what I ended up doing" is often the most interesting thing about a role.

**The people.** Direct and indirect reports. Built the team, inherited it, or both? Which hires mattered and why? Which other functions did they work with, and how did that actually go?

**The accomplishments.** For each one, push through to specifics:
- What did *you* specifically do, as distinct from what the team did?
- What was the business problem behind it?
- What was your approach, and what did you reject?
- What happened? Numbers: revenue, users, retention, cost, time, hiring, market share.

**The substance.** Tools and systems actually used. Methods introduced. Strategic calls made. What they built from nothing versus what they optimized.

**The hard parts.** What was genuinely difficult? What failed? What did they change course on? How did they handle the organizational politics? This is where interview answers come from, and candidates volunteer none of it unless asked directly.

**The ending.** Why did they leave? What would they say they left behind?

## Technique

**Push for numbers, gently and twice.** "You said growth — do you remember roughly what it went from and to?" An approximation beats nothing; "roughly tripled" is usable, "significant growth" is not. Never round up on their behalf, and never supply a number they did not say.

**Find the story.** "What are you proudest of there?" and "How would you explain what you did there in thirty seconds?" produce better material than any structured question.

**Follow the vague answer, don't accept it.** "Large team" means nothing. "Nine engineers across two squads" goes in a document.

**Notice the patterns.** Always the turnaround, always the zero-to-one, always the person who ends up owning the messy thing nobody else wants. Name the pattern back to them and see if it lands — it usually becomes the throughline of the profile.

**Do not interrogate.** This is a conversation, and a tired candidate gives worse answers. If a role is clearly painful, get what is needed and move on.

## German-specific material

None of this can be reconstructed later, and every German document skill depends on it.

**Every gap over three months.** Get the truthful explanation — Elternzeit, Weiterbildung, Sabbatical, Umzug nach Deutschland, Arbeitssuche. German recruiters scrutinize Lücken far more than American ones, and the explanation must be identical in the Lebenslauf, the Anschreiben, and the interview. Record the exact wording once, and reuse it verbatim forever.

**Why each role ended**, in a version that will not contradict the Arbeitszeugnis. It gets asked directly.

**German-language working experience**, precisely. Not the certificate — the reality. Meetings held in German, German-speaking clients, documentation written in German, code reviewed in German. This is worth far more than a CEFR label and belongs in the Anschreiben when it is true.

**The Abschlussnote.** German CVs include the final grade and its absence is noticed. Also capture the institution and country, so a foreign degree can be checked against anabin.

**Which Zeugnisse exist**, and for which roles. A missing Arbeitszeugnis for a recent role invites a question, and it is much better to know that now than while assembling a Mappe against a deadline.

## Writing it up

Compile into `shared/templates/profile.md` structure. Keep the candidate's own phrasing wherever it is good — verbatim quotes make better bullets than anything paraphrased. Keep approximate metrics rather than dropping them. Record strengths as evidence, never as self-assessment: "grew the team from 4 to 19 in eighteen months" belongs in the profile; "strong leader" does not.
